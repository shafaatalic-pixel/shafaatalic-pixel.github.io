Heat Kills on Moderate Days, Not Extreme Ones

By Md Shafaat Ali Choyon · builds & runs AI in production · Growth & health strategist · 5 min read

NYC Health tracks roughly 500 heat-related deaths a year in the city. The dangerous assumption isn't about the number, it's about which days cause it. Most people picture a record-breaking heat wave. The data says otherwise, and the gap between the two is where public health messaging keeps failing.

Why does heat kill more on moderate days than extreme ones?

Extreme heat events get the alerts, the news segments, the cooling-center press conferences. They deserve some of that attention, but not most of it. NYC Health's Heat-Related Deaths Report found that roughly 80% of heat deaths occur on moderately hot days, in the 82–94°F range, not during the dramatic extremes. Black New Yorkers are disproportionately affected, a gap driven by housing quality and unequal access to cooling, not biology.

The day that kills you is rarely the one that made the news.

This is a recognition problem before it's a resource problem. A 90-degree Tuesday doesn't trigger the same alarm as a heat advisory, so people don't change behavior, check on neighbors, or open a window fan they own but forgot to use. The moderate day is dangerous precisely because it doesn't feel dangerous.

The Same Insight, a $0 Budget

I built HSREP, a health initiative that reached 57,274 people at $0 ad spend, on exactly this insight applied to a different risk. The campaigns that outperform aren't the ones broadcasting the most dramatic version of a threat, they're the ones targeting the under-recognized version people have already dismissed as not-a-big-deal. A $0 budget can't win an attention war against a heat wave headline. It can win by naming the risk nobody's warning about: the ordinary day. That's what made HSREP's reach possible without paid media, the message filled a recognition gap instead of competing for attention share against something already loud. Heat messaging has the same opening. The extreme-heat alert already has attention. The moderate day doesn't, and that's exactly where 80% of the deaths are.

Target the Risk People Have Already Dismissed

This reframes what "high-impact" public health communication actually looks like. It's not the campaign that matches the scale of the threat with the scale of the message. It's the one that finds where perceived risk and actual risk have come apart, and closes that specific gap. 80% of heat deaths on moderate days is a distribution insight disguised as a weather statistic.

The biggest risk is rarely the one with the biggest headline, it's the one that stopped getting one.

Anyone running a public health or safety campaign should be asking the same question I asked at HSREP: where has the "obvious" danger absorbed all the attention, leaving the actual danger unguarded?

The short version

  • 80% of NYC's ~500 annual heat deaths happen on moderate days (82–94°F), not extreme heat events.
  • Black New Yorkers are disproportionately affected, a housing and cooling-access gap, not a biological one.
  • HSREP reached 57,274 people at $0 ad spend by targeting the under-recognized risk, not the dramatic one.
  • The highest-impact message closes the gap between perceived risk and actual risk, it doesn't chase the loudest headline.
