A hotline on a bar of soap: the object is the channel

By Md Shafaat Ali Choyon MPH · CHES® · MBA · MCIM · builds & runs AI in production · Growth & health strategist · 7 min read · July 26, 2026

Think about where a bar of soap lives. On the edge of a sink in a Dhaka flat, in a plastic dish by the tap in a village courtyard, beside the bathroom mirror in a house in Detroit. Nobody downloads it. Nobody has to remember where they put it. Someone in the house picks it up every day, and nobody thinks of it as media.

At Praava Health, where I led marketing and corporate sales, we printed a doctor hotline on the packaging of Lifebuoy soap, sold nationwide. Doctors on that line answered about 3,000 calls a day. The Lifebuoy case study records what we built. This essay is about the idea underneath it, because I think it travels to any market: before you build a new channel to reach people, look hard at what they already hold.

Why put a health hotline on a bar of soap?

Because a new channel has to win two things before it helps anyone: a place in the home, and a reason to be looked at again. An everyday object already has both, and you do not pay for each look.

Compare the usual options. An app has to be found, downloaded, opened and remembered, by people who may not have the data or the storage to spare. An advertisement has to be bought again every time someone sees it, and it is gone when the budget is. Packaging is printed once and then seen every time the product is used, for as long as the bar lasts. Nobody had to form a new habit to see our number. They only had to wash.

The real gift was timing. Illness does not book an appointment. It shows up at night, on a market day, when the nearest clinic is a bus ride away. A channel that asks people to go looking fails at exactly that moment. An object that is already sitting in the house does not. The number was there before anyone needed it, so it was there when they did.

A new channel has to earn a place in the home. An everyday object already has one.

The same logic works for places as well as things. Part of the playbook that grew Praava's corporate clients from 342 to about 1,400 was on-site activations: going to the workplaces where people already spent their day, instead of waiting for them to find a clinic.

The object lends you its meaning, so choose one that already means your ask

Everyone asks about reach first. I think fit matters more. A borrowed object carries its own reputation into your message, and that reputation either helps you or quietly works against you.

Lifebuoy is sold as a health soap, and it was the market leader. A doctor's number on a health soap reads as a natural next step: this brand cares about germs, and here is a doctor. The same number printed on a biscuit wrapper would read as an advertisement, and people would treat it like one.

I learned the reverse version of this lesson earlier, at SureCash, where I helped deliver a government stipend to about 10 million beneficiaries. There, the new object was mobile money itself, and it meant nothing yet to most of the people receiving it. Trust was the entire barrier. So we borrowed meaning from things that already had it. Automated voice calls explained how the money would arrive and how to cash it out, for people who might never read a leaflet. Cash-out points were mapped against where beneficiaries actually lived. And we ran activations at rural mosques, places people already trusted.

The rule I took from both: do not ask what object reaches the most people. Ask what object people already trust for the thing you are about to ask of them.

The number is the cheap part. The answering is the product.

Printing a phone number costs almost nothing. It is also a promise, and the cost of keeping it lands on the other end of the line.

Our line was not a recorded message or a tip sheet. Doctors triaged each caller and referred the complex cases to the nearest government facility. At about 3,000 calls a day, the math is unforgiving: even if those calls were spread evenly across twelve hours, that is 250 calls an hour, every hour, each needing a trained person. My free hotline staffing calculator does this math properly, peaks included.

I started my career in customer service, about 80,000 customer interactions in all. What I took from it is simple: people judge the whole organization by the voice that answers. A number that rings out does worse than no number at all, because it teaches people that help is not really there.

The US is learning this at national scale. The 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline received 23.3 million contacts between its launch in July 2022 and March 2026, according to KFF. In the same analysis, about 3 in 4 call centers in a 2025 survey reported staffing shortages, and KFF notes that uneven or uncertain funding may affect call center capacity. Getting a three-digit number into people's heads has proved easier than keeping enough people on the line to answer it.

Every call is also a map

A borrowed channel at national scale produces something no advertising campaign ever does: a record of where people are sick, and with what.

Because the hotline was printed on a soap sold nationwide, the calls could be read district by district. We mapped illness patterns district by district and shared the summary with the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), the government's health directorate. A marketing partnership between a soap and a primary care provider became a small public-health data contribution.

That only happens if you design for it. If you put a line on an everyday object, design the call log at the same time as the script: where the caller is, what they called about, where they were sent. Those few fields are what turn a busy phone line into evidence that a health system can plan with. Skip them and you have 3,000 conversations a day that disappear when the call ends.

When does borrowing an everyday object fail?

It fails in three ways. The object stops matching the ask. Nobody answers. Or the surface gets crowded. The first two I have covered. The third is the one almost nobody plans for, and the US is running a live test of it right now.

California law requires schools that serve grades 7 to 12 and issue student ID cards to print help lines on them. Under Education Code section 215.5, those cards must carry the National Domestic Violence Hotline (since October 1, 2020) and the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (since July 1, 2025), and schools may add a QR code to their county's mental health resources. From July 1, 2026, public schools must also print the Trevor Project's LGBTQ+ hotline, by phone and by text, as the California Department of Education told districts in April 2026.

The instinct is right. A student ID is the soap bar of a teenager's life: carried every day, never downloaded, already trusted as official. And each of those lines answers a real need. But a card is a small surface. My worry, as someone who has designed for that moment of need, is that every number added makes any single one harder to find when a frightened person has a few seconds and shaking hands. The soap carried one number with one job.

So the test I would apply in Dhaka or in Detroit is the same. One object, one ask. If you have five services to offer, print one number that routes to all five, and spend the money you saved on the people who answer it.

The short version

  • An everyday object already has a place in the home and gets looked at again for free; a new channel has to earn both.
  • The object lends you its meaning. A doctor's number on a health soap reads as care; the same number on the wrong object reads as an ad.
  • The number is cheap; the answering is the product. Our line took about 3,000 calls a day, and in the US about 3 in 4 surveyed 988 call centers reported staffing shortages.
  • Design the call log with the script. District-level illness data from our calls went to the DGHS.
  • It fails when the object gets crowded. California student IDs now carry several help lines; one object, one ask is the safer rule.

What do the people you serve already pick up every day, and what one number would you put on it?
