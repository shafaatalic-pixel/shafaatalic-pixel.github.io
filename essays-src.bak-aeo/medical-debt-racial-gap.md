41% of working-age Americans report medical bill problems or active medical debt. Split it by race and the gap sharpens: 56% of Black adults report medical or dental debt, versus 37% of White adults. This reads as a uniquely American story about insurance gaps. It isn't. It's the US version of a question every health system on earth has to answer, one way or another.

## The Question Every System Has to Answer

Every health system, regardless of how it's financed, eventually confronts the same structural question: when the system doesn't cover a cost, who absorbs it? In the US, the answer is disproportionately the patient, and disproportionately a Black patient. **56% versus 37%** isn't a gap in health status. It's a gap in who has the financial cushion to absorb a system's shortfall without it becoming debt.

> Medical debt isn't what happens when healthcare fails. It's what happens when someone has to be the shock absorber, and the system decided who that would be.

That framing matters because it shifts the fix. You don't solve medical debt by making care cheaper in the abstract, you solve it by deciding, deliberately, who in the value chain is allowed to pass cost downstream and who has to eat it.

## Bangladesh Answers the Same Question, Differently

Bangladesh, a country of roughly 170 million people, runs one of the highest out-of-pocket health expenditure burdens in the world, public reporting puts it in the range of roughly 73% of total health spending paid directly by households, among the highest of any country tracked. It's not that the US system is broken and Bangladesh's isn't, or vice versa. It's that "who absorbs the cost when the system doesn't cover it" gets resolved differently in each place, insurance-gap debt in one, direct household out-of-pocket payment at the point of care in the other, but it's the identical structural question underneath. Neither system has actually solved who bears the shortfall. Both have just chosen a different group, through a different mechanism, to bear it.

## Trace It to Who Captures Margin

At Praava Health, I fixed a lab margin that was stuck at 30% by ending a kickback-driven referral model, doctors were being paid to refer, which meant patients, not the value chain, were quietly absorbing the inflated cost. Closing that loop moved margin to 50% without raising what patients paid. That's the same mechanism behind medical debt at scale: dysfunction in health financing, a kickback pricing model, a household's uncovered bill, an insurance gap, almost always traces back to who's allowed to capture margin, and who's left holding the difference. **30% to 50%** only happened because I found where the margin was being captured wrongly and closed it.

> Fix the margin capture, and the debt problem often shrinks before you've spent a dollar on the patient side.
