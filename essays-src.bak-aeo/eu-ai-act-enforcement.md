On August 2, 2026, the EU AI Act moves into full enforcement. The penalty ceiling for prohibited practices, €35M or 7% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, clears GDPR's old 4% mark, and it applies to any AI product reaching an EU user regardless of where the company is headquartered.

## A higher ceiling, and nowhere to hide behind a mailing address

The extraterritorial reach is the part most teams underestimate. OpenAI, Google, Meta, and Anthropic are all in scope not because they operate in Brussels, but because their products reach someone who does. That single design choice turns a regional law into a global compliance floor for anyone building or deploying AI systems. Most organizations have treated this the way they treated early GDPR, as a legal-team problem to be handled with disclosures and a longer terms-of-service page. That approach does not survive an audit of a high-risk AI system. **78%** of organizations had taken only minimal compliance action as of mid-2026, with full enforcement weeks away.

> A law with GDPR's reach and a higher ceiling does not wait for anyone to feel ready.

## What real compliance looks like, I've rebuilt a margin model under regulatory pressure before

The lesson I keep returning to is from Praava Health, where the fix that mattered was never a policy memo. Lab margins were stuck near 30% because a kickback-driven referral model quietly dictated where tests got ordered, a structure that looked like normal business until you traced the incentive chain. Ending that model and rebuilding referral and pricing logic around transparent economics moved margins to 50%, alongside corporate clients growing from 342 to roughly 1,400 accounts, a 4x expansion. Nothing about that fix was cosmetic. It required redesigning the operating model, not writing a new disclosure. The EU AI Act rewards the same instinct: risk classification, human-oversight checkpoints, and audit logging have to be built into the pipeline itself, because a bolt-on legal review does not survive contact with a regulator asking how a decision was actually made.

## The gap is the opportunity, for about six more months

If 78% of organizations are behind, the ones who treat this as a product-architecture question, not a paperwork exercise, gain real separation while competitors scramble under threat of the **€35M** ceiling. That means logging every high-risk decision path, building human review into the workflow rather than around it, and being able to explain a system's behavior on demand, not after a complaint. Regulation of this scale rarely gets rolled back once it lands; it gets built around. The companies treating August 2 as a design deadline instead of a legal one are the ones still shipping in Q4.

> Compliance built into the architecture is cheaper than compliance bolted onto the outcome.
