Marketing budgets just posted their flattest year in a decade, stuck at 7.7% of revenue, while 95% of CMOs say the pressure to prove ROI keeps climbing. The bill for that mismatch showed up in a single number: average CMO tenure fell to 4.1 years, the lowest Spencer Stuart has recorded in over ten years.

## The tenure number nobody wanted

Four years and one month. That's how long the average Chief Marketing Officer now lasts in the seat, per Spencer Stuart's newest tenure tracking, cited in the CMO Survival Guide 2026. It isn't cyclical noise, it's the lowest figure the firm has recorded in more than a decade, and it lands exactly when marketing budgets have flatlined at **7.7% of revenue**, essentially unchanged year over year. Flat budgets paired with rising expectations punish tenure rather than protect it. Boards that once tolerated an 18-month runway for brand investment to show results now want quarterly proof, and 95% of CMOs report the pressure to demonstrate ROI has intensified, not eased. The role hasn't gotten easier; it's gotten shorter.

> A shrinking chair is still a chair, the question is what keeps you in it.

## What surviving the squeeze actually requires

I ran into this pressure directly, not from the marketing chair but from operations, as COO of a multi-entity clinical group under Laser Treat. The mandate wasn't growth for its own sake, it was margin and retention inside a fixed budget, the same trap now defining CMO tenure. Digitizing scheduling, intake, and reporting across every entity cut **$0.5M in operating costs** without a single announcement-worthy campaign, and patient retention rose **13%** because the operational friction that used to lose patients between visits simply stopped existing. No one measured that work in brand lift, they measured it in whether the numbers held up next quarter, which is exactly the standard boards now apply to marketing. The lesson transfers directly: in a flat-budget, prove-it-now environment, the fastest path to credibility is removing the drag that's quietly costing money nobody's tracking, not launching a bigger campaign.

## The job description that's actually changing

None of this means marketing has lost influence, it means the currency changed. The CMOs clearing four-plus years aren't the ones with the boldest campaigns, they're the ones fluent enough in the P&L to sit in the same room as the CFO and argue in the CFO's language. That's a skill shift, not a budget increase, and it's available to anyone willing to build it. **95%** of CMOs feeling the ROI pressure means it's now the baseline condition of the job, not an occasional headwind.

> The CMOs who last aren't surviving the budget, they're fluent in it.
