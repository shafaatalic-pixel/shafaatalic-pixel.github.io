The FDA authorized 86 new AI/ML medical devices in Q2 2026 alone. The headline number is real, but the more interesting number is buried inside it: for the first time, radiology, long the default home of medical AI, no longer dominates the list by itself. This is a regulatory-maturity story, not an AI-hype one.

## The Spread Is the News, Not the Count

Radiology accounted for 59 of those 86 clearances, still the largest single category, but no longer the whole story. Clearances now span cardiology, anesthesiology, dental, and pathology, categories where AI approval was rare or nonexistent a few years ago. **59 of 86** means roughly a third of new clearances now sit outside the category everyone still associates with "AI in medicine."

> The story was never whether AI could pass regulatory review. It's whether the regulatory system could learn to review AI across an entire hospital, not just one department.

That's the harder, slower, less-covered achievement, a regulatory apparatus building the muscle to evaluate AI claims across specialties with genuinely different risk profiles and evidentiary standards, rather than one narrow pathway getting reused.

## Clearance Isn't Reach, Infrastructure Is

I've lived this gap between capability and reach before. At Praava Health, we built 6-hour COVID traveler testing plus home sample collection from scratch, during the pandemic, when the science of testing already existed, the hard part was never the assay, it was the operational infrastructure that turned a validated capability into something a traveler could actually use within a workday. That's the same gap regulatory breadth closes for AI devices now. A cleared device in pathology or anesthesiology doesn't reach a patient until the operational infrastructure around it, workflow integration, clinician trust, reimbursement pathways, catches up to the clearance. Broader FDA authorization is necessary. It's not sufficient. The lesson from building diagnostic infrastructure under pressure is that the clearance and the operational build have to happen close together, or the capability sits unused.

## Watch the Categories, Not the Headline Count

The right way to read a number like 86 isn't as an AI adoption curve, it's as a maturity signal for the system evaluating AI. **Clearances now span four-plus specialties** in a single quarter, which says more about institutional readiness than about any single algorithm's performance.

> When a regulator stops treating every new specialty as a special case, that's when a technology has actually arrived.

For anyone building or evaluating health AI, the category spread, not the raw count, is the number to track quarter over quarter.
