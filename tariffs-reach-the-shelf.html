Tariffs reach the shelf in a year: price like you mean it

By Md Shafaat Ali Choyon MPH · CHES® · MBA · MCIM · builds & runs AI in production · Growth & health strategist · 6 min read · Jan 12, 2027

When the US raised tariffs in 2025, foreign exporters barely cut their prices. New research from the New York Fed, published on 6 October 2026, finds that nearly 90%1 of the tariffs passed through to US import prices, and that import prices rose almost one for one in the first month. Yet a 10%2 tariff on all imports raises consumer goods prices by only about 2.6% after twelve months. The cost hits a business in weeks. The shelf catches up over a year. What you do in between is your pricing strategy, whether you planned one or not.

How fast do tariffs reach consumer prices?

Slower than they reach your costs. The researchers find that about half of the direct effect on imported goods shows up in consumer prices within three months, and all of it within six. Then a second wave builds: the effect on US-made goods more than doubles between six and twelve months.

Part of the gap is simple. Distribution margins, the retail and wholesale share, make up about half of what a consumer pays. So a 10% jump in the cost of an imported item does not become a 10% jump at the till. By February 2026, tariffs were adding 2.9 percentage points to goods inflation. Without them, the researchers estimate, goods prices would have fallen slightly.

For an operator, that lag is the point. Your margin takes the hit first. Your customers notice later. The months in between are when you choose whether to absorb, pass on, or redesign.

Your domestic competitors will raise prices too

The least discussed finding is about goods made in America. About a third of the total price effect comes from them1, not from imports. Some of that is input costs. But the researchers also find that domestic producers raise their markups when the imports they compete with get more expensive.

That changes the question. If you sell against imports, a tariff gives you pricing cover, and your competitors are likely to use it. If you hold your price while everyone else moves, you are not being kind to customers. You are repositioning your brand as the cheap option, maybe without meaning to. I argued in Pricing is positioning that a price is a message. A tariff year is when that message gets rewritten for you if you do not write it yourself.

The rules will change again, so build a system, not a reset

Tariff policy has moved every few months. In early 2026, the Supreme Court ended the tariffs imposed under emergency powers, and a temporary 10% surcharge took their place. When that expired, the US imposed new Section 301 tariffs on 60 economies from 24 July 2026, including a 10% tariff on goods from Bangladesh, with some exclusions and a planned quota for certain textiles and apparel.

Bangladesh sold $3.86 billion3 of goods to the US in the first five months of 2026, according to US Census data reported by Bonik Barta. So the same policy shift squeezes a garment exporter in Bangladesh and a retailer in Michigan, from two ends of one supply chain.

When the rules change this often, a single big price increase is the wrong tool. You will either move too much, too early, or have to move again and lose trust. What works is a pricing system: a set review date, clear cost triggers, and a plan for which products move first.

Customers forgive a price change they can understand. They punish one they discover on their own.

Bangladesh has priced through inflation for years

Bangladesh's consumers have lived with high inflation for more than three and a half years. It was 8.34%4 in September 2026, according to the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics. The country's consumer brands have learned to price under pressure, and they rarely start with the sticker price.

In April 2026, when fuel prices jumped, PRAN-RFL told The Daily Star it was cutting trade margins and consolidating deliveries, and called a higher retail price "a last resort"5. Bombay Sweets said it raises prices or cuts weights for each product, never both at once. Shoppers moved too. Bonik Barta reports that for PRAN's biscuits, the contribution of small packs surged from 12% to 60%6, and Meghna Group's products priced at Tk 10 to 20 have grown about 25% since 2024.

That is the flip. US brands tend to treat price as one number to raise or hold. Bangladeshi brands treat it as a ladder: protect the price point people remember, then change the pack, the size or the bundle around it. I learned this at Samsung, where the J2 was our mass-market entry, and in a mass market the price point is the first thing the product says. And I learned the other half at Praava Health, where most patients paid out of pocket. When we replaced referral kickbacks with transparent pricing, lab margins rose from about 30% to 50%. Honest prices built trust, and trust carried volume.

One caution from Dhaka: a quietly smaller pack is a trust risk. Offer a new, smaller size at the old price point as a choice, and say what changed. That is pack design. Shrinking the old pack in silence is just a hidden increase.

Price like you mean it

If I were advising a US consumer brand or retailer this quarter, I would do five things:

  • Map exposure by product. Which items carry imported inputs, and how much of their cost is tariff? Not every SKU needs to move.
  • Use the lag. Costs arrive in weeks, the market moves over twelve months. Stage your changes instead of moving once and hard.
  • Build the ladder. Keep a clear entry price point, add a smaller or simpler option, and let the premium tier carry more of the increase.
  • Change one thing at a time, and tell customers why, in plain words. A short note beats a surprise at checkout.
  • Watch your competitors' moves. If domestic rivals raise markups, holding your price is a positioning choice. Make it on purpose.
