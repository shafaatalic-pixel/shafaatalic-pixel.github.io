AI Is Now Beating Human Drug Discovery on Its Own Numbers

By Md Shafaat Ali Choyon MPH · CHES® · MBA · MCIM · builds & runs AI in production · Growth & health strategist · 4 min read

More than 200 AI-designed drugs are now in clinical development. Their Phase I success rate runs 80-90%, against 40-65% for traditionally discovered drugs, and target-to-IND time has dropped from 3-5 years to 18 months. This is not AI writing lab notes faster. It's AI beating human-led discovery on the industry's own scorecard.

Is AI actually beating human drug discovery on accuracy?

Every technology promises speed. Fewer prove they improve the underlying hit rate. Cutting target-to-IND time from 3-5 years to 18 months is a logistics win, useful, but expected of automation. The number that should stop people is the Phase I success rate: 80-90% for AI-designed drugs versus 40-65% for the traditional pipeline. That is not compute doing paperwork faster. That is a fundamentally better filter for which molecular candidates deserve human and capital investment in the first place, catching failure modes earlier than a purely human-run discovery process typically does.

Speed changes the timeline. Accuracy changes which drugs are worth timing at all.

I've seen a locked measurement framework produce the same kind of multiplier

The mechanism behind that accuracy gap is familiar to me from a completely different domain. My MPH capstone documented a repeatable ~3x reach improvement, and the lever wasn't more content or more budget, it was a locked measurement framework that removed the guesswork from what "working" actually meant, so every iteration compounded on verified signal rather than noise. AI drug discovery is running the same play at industrial scale: instead of advancing candidates on partial, noisy preclinical signals the way human teams historically have, it screens against a locked, consistent evaluation framework across millions of molecular permutations. The multiplier in both cases comes from the same place, discipline in what counts as evidence, not more raw effort thrown at the problem.

The pipeline itself is being redesigned, not just accelerated

An 18-month target-to-IND timeline combined with near-double the Phase I success rate doesn't just produce more drugs, it changes which diseases are economically viable to pursue at all, including conditions too rare or too complex for a 5-year, high-attrition traditional pipeline to justify. Two hundred candidates already in clinical development is the proof point, not the ceiling.

A discovery process that fails less often is worth more than one that simply moves faster.

The short version

  • 200+ AI-designed drugs are already in clinical development.
  • Phase I success runs 80-90% for AI-designed drugs versus 40-65% traditionally.
  • Target-to-IND time dropped from 3-5 years to 18 months.
  • The gain is a locked evaluation framework, not just faster computation, a pattern that holds across domains.

If your process rewarded speed but never audited whether it also improved your hit rate, would you actually know?
