The EU blinked on AI. Use the extra 16 months to build, not wait

By Md Shafaat Ali Choyon MPH · CHES® · MBA · MCIM · builds & runs AI in production · Growth & health strategist · 6 min read · Oct 20, 2026

On 1 August I wrote that the EU AI Act's enforcement deadline was a redesign brief. What I missed: five days earlier, Brussels had moved it. The Digital Omnibus on AI, in force since 27 July 20261, pushed the rules for high-risk systems out by 16 months. Plenty of teams read that as permission to stop. I read it as the most useful gift a regulator has given AI builders this year: time to design the oversight in, instead of bolting it on in a panic.

What did the Digital Omnibus change in the EU AI Act?

The headline is the calendar. Rules for standalone high-risk systems, the ones used in hiring, education, credit scoring, health and life insurance pricing, access to public benefits and emergency triage, now apply from 2 December 2027 instead of 2 August 2026. AI built into products that already have EU safety rules, such as medical devices, moves to 2 August 20281.

It took one of the fastest passages Brussels has managed. Parliament adopted the deal on 16 June by 4232 votes to 57, the Council approved it on 29 June, and it was published in the Official Journal on 24 July. The Omnibus also eased paperwork for small and mid-sized companies and added one new ban, on AI that generates non-consensual intimate images or child abuse material2.

What did not move

Three things stayed exactly where they were, and they are the ones that bite first.

The bans have applied since February 2025: manipulation that causes harm, exploiting people's age, disability or money worries, social scoring, and reading emotions at work or in school. Breaking them can cost up to €35M or 7% of global turnover.

The transparency rules started on 2 August 2026. People must be told when they are talking to an AI, and AI-generated content must be marked. Systems that were already on the market before that date have until 2 December 20261 to add machine-readable marking. That is six weeks away.

And the reach did not change. The Act follows the user, not the headquarters. A team in Dhaka or Detroit whose product reaches people in the EU is in scope, wherever its servers sit.

A delay is not a discount. The fine for getting high-risk AI wrong is the same in 2027; you just get one chance to build it right first.

Why the delay helps builders, not laggards

Compliance done in a hurry is a legal patch: a disclosure page, a policy, a checkbox. Compliance done with time is architecture: a log of every decision, a named person who can override the system, a way for people to appeal. The first fails the moment a regulator asks how a decision was made. The second is just a well-run product.

I have seen that difference in money. At Praava Health, the fix for a broken referral model was not a policy memo. We rebuilt referral and pricing around transparent economics, and lab margins moved from about 30% to 50%. Building the honest version into the operating model was cheaper than defending the old one. The AI Act rewards the same instinct, and 16 extra months is enough to do it properly.

What I would build before 2 December 2027

If I ran an AI product that might be high-risk, here is the order I would work in:

  • Triage every use case now. Know which of your systems are banned, high-risk, transparency-only or minimal. My free AI use-case risk triage does a first pass in ten questions.
  • Ship the transparency rules first. They are already in force, and the marking deadline is 2 December 2026. Tell people when they are talking to an AI, and label what it makes.
  • Log decisions from day one. Input, output, model version and time, for every decision that matters to a person. You cannot add a history you never recorded.
  • Name a human owner. One person who answers for each system and can stop it.
  • Build the appeal path. A plain way for someone to question a result and reach a person. Colorado will require one for consequential decisions from 1 January 2027 anyway3.

This is not theory for me. Ask Shafaat, the AI assistant on this site, tells visitors it is an AI, keeps an anonymous log of every question and answer, and has an off switch that hands people to a guided advisor. It is a small system, and it still needed all three.

The short version

  • The Digital Omnibus, in force since 27 July 2026, moved high-risk AI rules to 2 December 2027, and to 2 August 2028 for AI inside regulated products.
  • The bans and their €35M or 7% fines have applied since February 2025 and did not move.
  • Transparency rules started on 2 August 2026, with until 2 December 2026 to mark AI content from systems already on the market.
  • The Act still follows the user, so teams in Bangladesh or the US serving people in the EU are in scope.
  • Use the 16 months to build logging, a human owner and an appeal path into the product, not to wait.

If a regulator asked tomorrow how your AI made one specific decision, could you show them, step by step?

Frequently asked questions

Did the EU delay the AI Act?

Partly. The Digital Omnibus on AI, in force since 27 July 2026, moved the rules for standalone high-risk AI systems from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and for AI built into regulated products such as medical devices to 2 August 2028. The bans and the transparency rules were not delayed.

When do the EU AI Act transparency rules apply?

From 2 August 2026. People must be told when they are interacting with an AI system, and AI-generated content must be marked. Systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 have until 2 December 2026 to add machine-readable marking.

Does the EU AI Act apply to companies outside the EU?

Yes. It applies when an AI system is placed on the EU market or its output is used in the EU, wherever the company is based, so a team in Bangladesh or the US serving people in the EU is in scope.
