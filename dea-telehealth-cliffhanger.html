Telehealth's Fourth Extension Shows Regulatory Uncertainty Is a Distribution Problem

By Md Shafaat Ali Choyon MPH · CHES® · MBA · MCIM · builds & runs AI in production · Growth & health strategist · 5 min read

The DEA and HHS just extended pandemic-era telemedicine flexibilities for prescribing controlled substances, ADHD medication, some addiction treatment, through 2026. It's the fourth temporary extension in a row. No permanent rule has replaced it. For an industry that needs multi-year investment horizons, a fourth cliffhanger isn't a policy footnote. It's the policy.

Is telehealth prescribing of controlled substances still allowed?

Telehealth companies, clinicians, and patients relying on controlled-substance prescribing via telemedicine have now built four years of operational planning on temporary ground. Four consecutive extensions, zero permanent rules, tells providers something specific: build for this channel, but don't build as if it's stable. That's a hard instruction to act on, and an easy one to under-invest against.

An extension isn't the same thing as a policy. It's a promise to decide later, renewed just often enough that nobody stops depending on it.

The practical effect is chilling investment at the margin, telehealth platforms hesitant to expand controlled-substance prescribing capacity, clinicians hesitant to build a patient panel around a rule that could lapse, patients uncertain whether a prescribing relationship established this year survives into next.

Bangladesh's Version of the Same Access Question

Bangladesh, a market of roughly 170 million people with uneven physician distribution outside major cities, faces its own version of this exact tension, a fast-growing Bangladeshi diaspora increasingly using telehealth to consult with doctors back home, alongside an emerging domestic telehealth prescribing framework still being defined. In both markets, the constraint isn't demand or technology, it's whether the regulatory ground underneath telehealth is stable enough for providers to build durable services on top of it. A diaspora patient consulting a Dhaka physician by video, or a US patient renewing an ADHD prescription through a telehealth platform, both depend on the same thing: a rule that isn't due to expire again in six months. Regulatory certainty, or its absence, determines whether telehealth becomes core infrastructure or stays a workaround.

Uncertainty Is Itself a Distribution Problem

I built distribution for SureCash's government stipend program, reaching roughly 10 million unbanked people with no bank branch access, that scale was only possible because the regulatory and institutional rules underneath mobile money were stable enough for the market to build confidently on top of them. 10 million people reached through a channel nobody had to keep re-approving. Access infrastructure, telehealth prescribing rules here, financial services distribution rails there, only works at population scale when the rules underneath it are settled enough for the market to commit real capital and real trust to it.

You can't build a 10-million-person distribution channel on a rule that expires every few months, the uncertainty itself becomes the bottleneck, not the technology.
