$11.1B and no IPOs: digital health is now an execution business

By Md Shafaat Ali Choyon MPH · CHES® · MBA · MCIM · builds & runs AI in production · Growth & health strategist · 6 min read · Dec 31, 2026

US digital health startups raised $3.7B1 across 114 deals in the third quarter of 2026, which takes the year to $11.1B1 across 358 deals. That is real money. But no digital health company has gone public since HeartFlow in August 2025, and the one that tried last month pulled out. When capital keeps coming and the exit door stays shut, the business changes. Digital health is no longer a story you sell to the next investor. It is an operation you have to run.

Is digital health funding recovering in 2026?

On the surface, yes. Rock Health, which has tracked the sector for fifteen years, notes that 2026 could pass $14.8B if the pace holds. For scale, the whole sector raised $1.2B in 2011.

Look closer and the money is concentrating. The average deal so far this year is $31.0M1, up from $29.5M in 2025 and $20.6M in 2024. Bigger checks, fewer companies. Rock Health's own reading is that building software is no longer the hard part, because APIs and generative AI make it cheap. What separates companies now is implementation, integration and evidence. Health systems are tired of pilots and point solutions. They want fewer vendors that fit together.

I wrote earlier about zero digital health IPOs in six months. The new development is that the drought now runs past a year, and the market has started to tell even strong companies to wait.

The exit is now a merger, not a listing

On 29 September, Oura, the smart ring company, postponed a planned $2.1 billion2 IPO, citing uncertainty in the market, despite about $1.4 billion in revenue over the prior twelve months. If a company that size waits, a Series B company should not plan around a listing.

Mergers are where the exits are. The sector logged 45 acquisitions in the third quarter and 1641 so far this year, about level with 2025's pace. An acquirer buys something that already works inside a customer's walls: a product that is installed, used and paid for. That is a different thing to build than a product that demos well.

When the exit door is shut, the operation is the product. Build the company an acquirer would want to run, not the one an investor would want to hear about.

Bangladesh never had the exit, so it built the operation

Here is what the US market is learning that Bangladesh's operators have always known. In the first half of 2026, startups in Bangladesh raised $6 million3 in total, down 95% from $120 million a year earlier, across just six deals. Domestic investors put in 0%3 of it. In 2025 the country's startups raised $124 million4, but about 89% of that came from a single merger deal.

Since 2013, about 98%3 of the country's late-stage startup money has come from foreign investors. There is no deep local market to sell into or list on. So the health companies that survive are built to pay for themselves. At Praava Health, where I led marketing and corporate sales, most patients paid out of pocket. Growth of about 45% a year from 2021 to 2023 came from patients and corporate clients who had to see the value before they paid. Every new service had to work in the clinic on Monday, not in a pitch deck.

That is the flip. US founders are treating "execution business" as a new condition. In Bangladesh it was always the only condition, and it produced operators who know how to make a health business run on its own cash.

What execution actually looked like

As COO at Laser Treat, a multi-entity clinical group in Bangladesh, I ran customer service, clinical operations, business functions and finance. There was no product launch to point to. The work was integration and process: an ERP to put operations on one system, and business intelligence so managers saw the same numbers each week.

The result was about $0.5M lower operating cost and about 13% better patient retention. Neither number would excite a venture investor in 2021. Both are exactly what an acquirer checks in 2026, because they show a company that runs well without new money.

This is also where AI earns its place. I build and run agentic AI in production, and the useful work is rarely the flashy demo. It is reconciling records, chasing follow-ups and flagging the patient who did not come back. That is the same integration and process work, done at a lower cost per patient. Rock Health's point about cheap building cuts both ways: if anyone can build the feature, the moat is how well you run it.

How to run a health company when no one is coming

If I were advising a digital health company today, I would plan on five things:

  • Assume no listing for 24 months. Plan the next two years on your own cash plus one round, not two. My free cash runway calculator shows how many months you really have.
  • Show the revenue that repeats. Acquirers pay for contracts that renew. My free recurring revenue bridge separates new, expanded, lost and kept revenue so you can see which one is moving.
  • Sell integration, not features. Health systems want fewer vendors. Be the one that plugs into what they already run.
  • Track cost to serve per patient. If AI lowers it, prove it with a number each month.
  • Count retention before growth. At Laser Treat, lifting patient retention about 13% was worth more than any campaign.

The short version

  • US digital health raised $11.1B in the first nine months of 2026, across 358 deals, with an average deal of $31.0M.
  • No digital health IPO since August 2025; Oura postponed a $2.1 billion listing in September.
  • Mergers are the exit: 164 so far in 2026, so build what an acquirer wants to run.
  • Bangladesh startups raised $6 million in the first half of 2026, so its health operators learned to run on their own cash.
  • Execution is the moat: integration, process, retention and cost to serve, with AI doing the routine work.
